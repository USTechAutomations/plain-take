Reader tips for self-tapes (and what to do with no reader)

A reader's job on a self-tape is small on purpose: give you something real to react to without pulling focus away from you. Here is how to set that up, and what to do when you are taping alone.

What a reader's job actually is

A reader delivers the other lines at a normal, clear pace so you have something real to respond to. They are not there to act the scene, compete with your energy, or add their own performance choices.

The best readers sound almost neutral. If a reader is memorable, they are probably pulling focus away from you.

Where to put the reader

Stand the reader as close to the lens as possible, on whichever side you decide your eyeline will land, and keep them there for the whole scene so your eyeline does not drift take to take.

If the reader has to move for some reason, tell them ahead of time so you know where to keep your eyes.

Keeping reader volume down

Ask the reader to keep their voice slightly quieter than yours, especially if they are close to the same microphone. A reader who is louder than you can bury your lines on playback.

Do a short sound check with the actual microphone setup before the full take, so you catch this before it becomes a problem on the tape you send.

Giving a reader quick direction

Before you start, tell the reader the pace you want and anything specific about tone, in one or two sentences. A reader who understands the shape of the scene gives you better cues than one reading cold.

Do not over-direct. A reader who is thinking about their own performance stops being a clean, simple cue for yours.

Taping alone with no reader

If nobody is available, a phone or a laptop can play a pre-recorded reader track while you perform against it, as long as the timing does not force your pace.

Note cards with the other character's cue lines, glanced at quickly near the lens, also work when a recording is not practical.

Choosing who to ask

A roommate, a friend, or a fellow actor in your class all make fine readers. The best choice is whoever will show up on time, stay off-script, and read at a steady pace without needing constant direction.

If the same person reads for you often, they will get better at it, so it is worth asking the same one or two people rather than a new stranger every time.

Adjusting your eyeline when reading solo

Whether you use a recording or note cards, keep your eyeline fixed at one mark near the lens for the whole scene, the same way you would with a live reader standing there.

A shifting eyeline is one of the fastest ways a solo tape starts to look unfocused, even when the performance itself is strong.
